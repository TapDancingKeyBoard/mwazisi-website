How digitally ready is a small business? Assessing SMEs for growth and export

"Going digital" means very different things to a market trader and to a manufacturer selling across borders. Here is how we assess where a small business really stands, and what that looks like in practice.

Sam Gondwe, Principal Consultant · October 2026 · 3 min read

In 2026 we served as the digital specialist on a regional research programme studying how small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and the institutions that support them prepare for trade and export. Our job was to make sure digital readiness was measured properly and built into the wider analysis, alongside finance, inclusion and climate resilience.

The findings belong to the programme, so this article doesn't discuss them. It covers the approach, which works for any organisation that needs to understand how digital the businesses it supports really are.

What a digital specialist does on a study like this

  • Defines what to measure. We developed digital adoption indicators and a scorecard so that "digital readiness" could be compared consistently across businesses and sectors.
  • Shapes the research tools. We added digital questions to the surveys and interview guides, and adapted them to local context.
  • Talks to people. We ran key-informant interviews and focus group discussions with businesses, trade support institutions and other stakeholders.
  • Analyses and reports. We contributed digital-specific analysis at each stage, from the initial analysis through to the final report.
  • Tests the conclusions. We took part in validation workshops where businesses, associations and regulators review the findings before they are final.

A simple digital maturity ladder

Most businesses don't fall neatly into "digital" or "not digital". We find it more useful to think in steps. This is our own simplified version, not the programme's scorecard:

  1. 1
    Reachable. Customers can find and contact the business by phone, WhatsApp or social media.
  2. 2
    Visible. A website or business profile, consistent branding and an email address on its own domain.
  3. 3
    Running digitally. Accounting software, cloud email and files, mobile money or card payments, and backups.
  4. 4
    Selling digitally. Online orders, marketplaces, digital invoices and payments that work across borders.
  5. 5
    Using data. Sales and customer data guide decisions on stock, pricing and which markets to target.

The value is in the next step. A business on step 2 rarely needs an e-commerce platform; it usually needs reliable email, simple bookkeeping and a way to take payments.

Why interviews matter as much as surveys

Surveys tell you whether a business uses a tool. Conversations tell you why not: the cost of data, an unreliable connection, not trusting online payments, or simply not knowing where to start. Hearing these reasons first-hand is what turns a score into practical recommendations, for businesses and for the institutions that support them.

Support institutions count too. Chambers of commerce, sector associations and export agencies are often a business's first source of advice. Their own digital capacity shapes how well they can help their members.

Questions any small business can ask itself

  • Can a new customer find us online and get a reply the same day?
  • Do we send professional invoices, and do we know who owes us money?
  • Can we accept payment the way our customers want to pay, including from another country?
  • If a laptop or phone was lost tomorrow, would we lose our records?
  • Does more than one person know how our key systems and accounts work?
  • Which single digital step would save us the most time or win us the most sales this year?

What this means for programmes and partners

For anyone designing support for SMEs, the lesson is to measure before prescribing. A clear picture of where businesses stand, gathered with consistent indicators and checked in conversation, leads to support that fits: training where skills are the gap, tools where processes are, and policy dialogue where the barriers are outside the business's control.
