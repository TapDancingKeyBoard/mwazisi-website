Nine months of managed IT support for an NGO country office

What a monthly IT retainer actually covers, from laptop rebuilds to choosing an internet provider, based on our work with the Zambia office of an international non-profit.

Sam Gondwe, Principal Consultant · October 2026 · 5 min read

In January 2026 we began providing IT support to the Lusaka country office of an international non-profit organisation. Like many NGOs, the organisation runs its core systems centrally from its head office abroad. What the local team needed was someone on the ground: a person who knows the office, its people and its equipment, and who can work alongside the head-office IT team when something breaks.

This article describes what that work looked like over the first nine months. We have left out anything that would identify the organisation or its staff.

How the arrangement works

  • A monthly retainer. The office pays a predictable monthly fee instead of calling someone in, and paying a call-out charge, every time something goes wrong.
  • Agreed service categories. Every piece of work is logged against a category agreed at the start: device support, application support, network and connectivity, advisory, platform support, and security and access.
  • A written timesheet each month. Each entry says what was done, for whom and how long it took. It goes to the client with the invoice, so there is never a question about what they are paying for, and it doubles as an audit trail for donor reporting.
  • On-site and remote. Regular visits to the office, with remote support in between.
  • One point of contact. We liaise with the head-office IT team, the internet service providers and equipment suppliers, so local staff don't have to.

Starting with orientation

The first weeks were spent understanding the environment rather than fixing things: which applications staff use, how devices are managed, where the head office's responsibilities end and the local office's begin. That context made every later job faster.

Orientation now also runs the other way. When new staff or interns join, we give them a short IT induction based on the organisation's IT policy, covering acceptable use, security practices and how to get help.

What the work looked like

Between January and September we logged about 150 entries. Most were short, practical jobs on staff laptops and printers, which is typical for an office of this kind.

Devices

Laptops were the biggest single area of work: rebuilding machines from a clean operating system image, moving staff onto new computers along with their files and applications, upgrading memory from 8 GB to 16 GB to extend the life of older laptops, enrolling devices in central device management, checking battery health, and diagnosing machines that would no longer start.

Applications

Typical jobs included fixing Microsoft Office activation and Outlook problems, repairing damaged spreadsheets, installing business applications through the organisation's self-service company portal, and moving a user's data out of a retired virtual desktop system before it was switched off.

Networks

There were fewer network jobs, but they tended to affect everyone at once, so they mattered more. We cover two of them below.

Advice and documentation

Some of the most useful work produced documents rather than repairs: an IT inventory of every device in the office, specifications for new equipment purchases, an internet provider comparison, and compliance, backup and recovery documentation aligned with head-office requirements.

Three examples

1. Office documents opening in the wrong program

Staff found that Word and Excel files had started opening in a different, free office suite that had been installed on some machines. Formatting broke and files saved in the wrong format. We removed the other suite and restored Microsoft 365 as the default program for every Office file type. It's a small fix, but it had been costing people time every day.

2. A Wi-Fi outage with more than one cause

When the office lost its wireless network, the internet provider, the head-office IT team and our own checks each pointed at a different possible cause. We coordinated the three, restored the wireless router's configuration and found a faulty network cable running to the wireless access point. Replacing the cable brought the network back in full.

3. Choosing an internet provider

The office was offered a mobile operator's business package as an alternative to its existing dedicated fibre line. We prepared a side-by-side comparison explaining the difference between a dedicated connection, where the bandwidth is reserved for you, and a contended one, where it is shared with other customers. The comparison also covered guaranteed speeds, service-level agreements and support terms. Later, working with the provider and the head-office team, we measured the speeds the office was actually getting, so the decision could rest on numbers rather than on the sales sheet.

Small fixes add up. A scanner that couldn't "see" its laptop turned out to be on a different Wi-Fi network. A printer whose network port had failed was moved onto wireless. A naming and fixed-address plan for the office printers means staff now pick the right printer first time.

What we would tell any NGO office

  • Keep an up-to-date IT inventory. It makes procurement, insurance, audits and staff handovers far easier.
  • Write everything down. A monthly log of what was done is useful for budgeting, for donor reporting and for spotting problems that keep coming back.
  • Extend before you replace. A memory or SSD upgrade often gives an older laptop another year or two of useful life for a fraction of the cost of a new one.
  • Standardise the boring things. One Wi-Fi network profile and consistent printer names and addresses prevent a surprising number of support calls.
  • Induct new staff on IT. Twenty minutes on day one about policy, security and where to get help saves hours later.
  • Agree who does what with head office. Local and central IT work best together when each knows clearly where its responsibilities start and end.
