Dedicated or shared internet? How to choose a business connection

Two offers can show the same speed on paper and perform very differently on a Monday morning. Here is what the difference is, what to ask your provider, and how to check what you are really getting.

Sam Gondwe, Principal Consultant · October 2026 · 4 min read

When an office's internet feels slow, the first instinct is usually to buy a faster package. Often the real issue is not the headline speed but the type of connection: whether the bandwidth is reserved for you or shared with other customers. We regularly help organisations in Lusaka compare internet offers, and this is the question that matters most.

The difference in one picture

Shared (contended) connections

Most home and small-business packages are shared. The provider sells a headline speed, "up to" a certain number of megabits per second, but the line out of your area is shared with other customers. When they are busy, everyone's speed drops. The ratio of customers to capacity is called the contention ratio, and providers don't always publish it.

Dedicated connections

A dedicated line, sometimes called dedicated internet access (DIA), reserves a set amount of bandwidth for your organisation alone. The speed you buy is the speed you get, at any time of day. Upload speed is usually the same as download speed, and the contract normally includes a service level agreement (SLA) with targets for uptime and repair times.

Which one do you need?

A shared connection is often perfectly adequate. Pay for dedicated capacity when slow or unreliable internet costs you real work, for example when:

  • staff spend much of the day on video calls, and calls freeze at busy times;
  • your finance, HR or programme systems run in the cloud or at a head office abroad;
  • you upload large files, such as reports, photos, survey data or backups;
  • you need a fixed IP address for remote access, a VPN or a security system;
  • an outage of a day or more would be serious, so you need a contract that commits the provider to a repair time.

Many organisations end up with a mix: a dedicated line as the main connection and a cheaper shared or mobile connection as a backup that takes over automatically when the main line fails.

Questions to ask any provider

  • Is this connection dedicated or shared? If shared, what is the contention ratio?
  • What are the guaranteed download and upload speeds, not just the maximum?
  • What does the SLA promise? Ask for the uptime target, the repair time, and what compensation applies if they miss it.
  • How do I report a fault, and is support available outside office hours?
  • Is a fixed IP address included?
  • What equipment is installed, and who maintains it?
  • How long is the contract, and what are the exit terms?

Check what you are really getting

Before you change provider or upgrade, measure what you have. It takes a few minutes a day for a week:

  • Run a speed test from a computer plugged into the router with a cable, not over Wi-Fi, so you measure the line rather than the wireless network.
  • Test at different times: early morning, mid-morning and mid-afternoon. On a shared line, the gap between quiet and busy times tells you a lot.
  • Record download, upload and latency (ping) each time, in a simple spreadsheet.
  • Compare the results with what your contract promises, and share them with your provider if they fall short.

Check the Wi-Fi too. In our experience, a good share of "slow internet" complaints turn out to be weak Wi-Fi, an overloaded router or a faulty cable rather than the provider's line. If tests on a cable are fine but staff still struggle, look at the office network first.

The short version

Shared internet is cheaper and fine for light use. Dedicated internet costs more but gives you the speed you pay for, all day, with commitments in writing. Measure what you have, ask the questions above, and choose based on how your team actually works.
